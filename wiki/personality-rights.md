# Personality rights

Generative tools can reproduce a particular person's style closely enough to substitute for their
work, and the objection to doing so is not primarily a legal one. That is the whole argument behind
the single prohibition inside the Four S's: in the Style field, never name a living artist.

Copyright is the obvious guess and the weak one. A style is a **methodology, not content**, and a
claim built on it would be, in the instructor's phrase, a very long war. The honest reading is that
you would probably not lose. It is worth sitting with how little that settles, because the same
sentence is available for almost every use of these tools, and treating it as permission is the
mistake the whole lecture is written against.

The real objection is commercial substitution. An image made in the manner of a living painter
**can be sold**. Every sale of one is a sale that artist did not make, and the thing being used is
not a file they own but the recognisable fact of being them — their personality rights, and with
them their credibility and their livelihood. The harm does not require copying anything; it
requires only that a buyer accept the imitation instead.

A dead artist is a grey area rather than a white one. Picasso is available in the sense that nobody
is losing a living, and the instructor's guidance stops at experimentation rather than shipping.
Grey is the accurate word: the substitution argument weakens, and it does not vanish.

The shape of this reasoning is the reusable part. The legal question is the weaker question. The
answer sits in what the output does to a real person. And "probably not illegal" is not the bar
anywhere in this lecture — not for an invented amenity in a drone shot, and not here.
